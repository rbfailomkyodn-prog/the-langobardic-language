The Langobardic language

The Langobardic language (Germanic) is spoken today by a small number of individuals in parts of Switzerland and Italy, more specifically, southeast of the Canton of Neuchâtel (Switzerland) and in the region of Bergamo (Italy). Although Langobardic has been considered extinct for a long period of time, it has re-emerged as a living, spoken language through a process known as Uuidarburt.

Its recovery became possible through the study of historical texts, linguistic research, comparative analysis, and Langobardic words preserved in other languages. Through Uuidarburt, Langobardic has been recovered and is used today for everyday communication and the expression of new ideas.

Closely related to other Ingvaeonic languages, Langobardic is classified by the Langobardic linguistic authority, Uuidarburt, as High Ingvaeonic. This term describes the language’s particular development from a North Sea Germanic language toward phonological characteristics associated with High German.

Uuidarburt

Uuidarburt is the methodology through which Langobardic is recovered, spoken, and developed. It brings together the study of the language, its formal description, and its everyday use, with speakers participating directly in its continued development.

The work involves examining historical and linguistic evidence, assessing earlier research, and bringing the findings together into a coherent description of Langobardic. At the same time, speakers use the language in conversation and in their own thoughts, drawing on their knowledge of it to express themselves, including when new expressions are needed.

When questions arise in speaking or when speakers propose new forms, these are considered through the methodology rather than adopted automatically. Speakers discuss the proposals, examine the relevant evidence, and determine through an established process whether changes should become part of the formal description. In this way, the language’s continued development is guided by both its use and the rigorous examination of its forms.

Speakers and the development of the language

Participation in Uuidarburt is not limited to speaking Langobardic. Speakers contribute through their use of the language, their observations, their proposals, and their involvement in discussing questions about its forms. They may develop an expression through their own familiarity with Langobardic, compare it with relevant linguistic evidence, and bring it forward for consideration.

As speakers communicate, their usage may converge and shared varieties emerge. A variety reflects the form of Langobardic spoken by its participants at a given moment, whether by one person or several. Newer varieties arise as the language develops, and convergence between speakers is an integral part of this process. Individual usage contributes to this development, while changes to the formal description are considered collectively through the methodology.

Uuidarburt thus connects the recovery of Langobardic with the people who speak it today. The study of the language, its use in everyday life, and the considered development of its formal description are parts of one continuing practice through which Langobardic lives and develops.

Methodology

1

Evidential Layer — Data Recovery

The Evidential Layer consists exclusively of research and the recovery of potentially relevant linguistic evidence. No reconstruction is established at this stage; the purpose is solely to identify, collect, document, and evaluate material that may contribute to the reconstruction.

The principal sources include, among others:

  • linguistic and historical-linguistic scholarship, including works by scholars such as W. Bruckner, K. Meyer, T. Vennemann, and others;
  • fossilized words, expressions, and morphological elements preserved in later or modern Italo-Romance varieties;
  • written records, historical attestations, and other potentially relevant documentary material;
  • previously overlooked or insufficiently explored linguistic research and evidence.

The objective of this layer is therefore exhaustive recovery rather than interpretation. Potentially useful evidence is collected so that it may subsequently be subjected to systematic analysis.

2

Analytical Layer — Analysis

The Analytical Layer concerns the critical examination of the material recovered at the Evidential Layer, together with existing claims made by linguists and hypotheses developed by ourselves within the reconstruction.

At this stage, fossilized forms, written evidence, proposed linguistic developments, and theoretical interpretations are examined in order to determine whether they should be incorporated into, modified within, or excluded from the reconstructed language. A substantial proportion of the rules identified in previous linguistic scholarship can be transferred to the Formalization Layer with relatively little discussion, insofar as they rest on established or strongly supported evidence. Greater attention is reserved for inferences, interpretations, and competing explanations whose validity is not self-evident.

The principal methodological alternatives at this layer are therefore: retention, correction, or rejection.

Where appropriate, competing analyses may be debated extensively before a determination is made. The result of this process is a body of linguistic rules and conclusions sufficiently justified to be considered for formal incorporation into the language.

3

Formalization Layer — Formalization of the Language

At the Formalization Layer, the results of the preceding analysis are incorporated into Langobardic as a coherent, internally structured, and fully usable linguistic system.

The language is not treated here merely as a collection of reconstructed forms. Rather, its phonology, morphology, syntax, lexicon, and other relevant features are formalized so that the language can be consistently learned and productively employed. Newly recovered material, newly established rules, and justified modifications are incorporated directly into the formal system.

At present, the principal contributors to this linguistic reconstruction are R. Uualander and R. Gualandris, who are currently engaged in the formalization of the linguistic system as a whole. This ongoing work constitutes the first version of the database and reflects the present stage of development of the formalized language; the formalization process itself is not regarded as being intrinsically assigned to these individuals, but is presently being carried out by them.

The Formalization Layer therefore constitutes the normative linguistic framework: it is the point at which analytical conclusions cease to be merely proposals and become operative components of the reconstructed language.

4

Operational Layer — Language Usage

The Operational Layer concerns the productive use of Langobardic by participants in everyday linguistic activity. Its purpose is not merely to demonstrate that the formalized system can generate individual forms, but to develop and test genuine linguistic competence through continued internalization and use.

This layer has two complementary components: internalization and conversation.

Internalization does not require an interlocutor. A participant must be capable of thinking, commenting internally, formulating personal reflections, and otherwise producing Langobardic independently. When a participant wishes to express or translate a thought for which no established Langobardic element is yet available, the specialized method is applied first: the missing element is inferred on the basis of the participant's internalized Langobardic linguistic patterns. This initial reconstruction serves as the primary candidate and may subsequently function as a secondary diagnostic indicator, for example in evaluating the strength and reliability of the participant's current Langobardic intuition.

Established rules are then applied. The relevant developments from Proto-West Germanic to Langobardic are followed, together with any additional Langobardic-specific developments required to derive the final reconstructed form.

Where established rules do not permit a sufficiently clear reconstruction, a comparative procedure is employed. A relevant comparative framework is established for the element in question, ordinarily including Proto-Germanic, Old Saxon, Old Frisian, Old English, Old High German, and any other languages of demonstrable relevance. Related forms are examined and their historical plausibility assessed. The specialized method is then applied to infer the element according to internalized Langobardic patterns, producing a primary reconstruction candidate. This candidate is subsequently compared with the forms identified through comparative analysis, and the evidence from both procedures is used to determine the final form.

Conversation, by contrast, concerns interaction between participants. In conversation, participants rely primarily upon their internalized linguistic patterns and intuition, conducting the interaction exclusively in Langobardic. Through continued use, the language may undergo a degree of natural self-regulation, as participants spontaneously adjust their usage to one another and recurrent patterns emerge through interaction.

Such spontaneous adjustments do not, however, automatically become formal rules. Where a disagreement arises, or where a novel pattern appears to require explicit recognition, the matter is formulated as a proposal and returned to the Analytical Layer. It is then subjected to analysis and discussion by the participants. Only after the proposal has been debated and approved through the established decision-making process may it be admitted as a new rule at the Formalization Layer.

The four methodological layers thus form a continuous methodological cycle rather than a strictly linear sequence. The Evidential Layer provides the evidence; the Analytical Layer determines its validity and significance; the Formalization Layer incorporates the resulting conclusions into the linguistic system; and the Operational Layer tests, internalizes, and potentially generates further developments within that system. In this way, the reconstructed language remains simultaneously evidence-based, historically grounded, formally coherent, and capable of productive use.

About us

Who we are

The Western European Linguistic Observatory documents the minority languages that official maps and statistics rarely show.

Our mission

The Western European Linguistic Observatory (WELO) is an independent initiative dedicated to observing and documenting the “invisible” minority languages of Western Europe: languages and varieties that are small in number of speakers, absent from public discourse, or overlooked by conventional linguistic surveys.

By bringing these languages into view, WELO aims to give a more comprehensive outlook on Europe’s linguistic realities, one that reflects not only its major national languages but the full diversity of the way its people speak.

What we do

1

Observe

We identify and follow minority languages across Western Europe, including those that are little known, endangered, or emerging.

2

Document

We record each language rigorously, drawing on historical sources, linguistic scholarship, and the testimony and usage of its speakers.

3

Share

We present our findings in a clear, accessible form so that researchers, institutions, and the public can discover these languages.

Our approach

WELO’s work is evidence-based and respectful of the communities involved. We give priority to primary sources and peer-reviewed research, we distinguish clearly between what is attested and what is reconstructed, and we recognise speakers as active participants in the life of their languages.

Our documentation covers living communities of speakers as well as languages that are being recovered, and each language we observe is presented with the same care, clarity, and rigor.

Our vision

A Europe whose linguistic diversity is fully seen, understood, and valued.
